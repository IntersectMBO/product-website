**About Zivana Protocol**

Zivana Protocol is trust infrastructure for the African informal economy. It is designed to make economic capability visible, verifiable, and financeable for informal economy participants without requiring them to enter formal systems first. It is built as five composable primitives that function as a single, integrated system.

A participant establishes a portable, self-sovereign identity anchored on a public ledger. No central platform controls issuance or can revoke it. That identity accumulates a trust score across cooperative and trading activity. The score stays private. The participant can prove to any counterparty that it clears a required threshold without revealing the underlying value.

When capital is committed, a covenant holds it and releases it only under the exact conditions everyone agreed to upfront. Both parties can verify the terms were met. Distributions move to recipients via stablecoin rails at near-zero cost. Revenue events are attested on-chain so that capital providers anywhere in the world can independently verify what was earned, without contacting the participant or trusting any intermediary.

An autonomous intelligence layer fetches real-world market data, packages it as a verifiable on-chain fact statement, and makes it available to other protocol primitives and smart contracts so that decisions are grounded in independently checkable evidence.

Together these five primitives allow a trader in Lagos to build a portable economic identity, access aligned capital, prove performance, and receive distributions, while a capital provider in any location can verify every step of that chain on a public ledger without trusting any single institution.

The six validation exercises in this document prove that each component of this stack works on real infrastructure. They are not proofs of concept. They are evidence that the engineering decisions underlying Zivana Protocol hold up when tested against live networks, real fees, real failure modes, and real constraints. Where components have limitations, those limitations are documented precisely so that the confidence level attached to each finding is honest.

**Treatment of Evidence in This Document**

All six validation cases are treated as applicant-supplied conflicted evidence throughout this research. NexTrium Global Innovations Ltd is the entity that both developed Zivana Protocol and conducted these validations. The findings documented here represent our own engineering work tested against our own architectural decisions. They are not independent evidence.

This does not make the findings less real. Every claim in this document is backed by verifiable on-chain transactions, public GitHub repositories, and reproducible test suites. But it does mean these findings occupy a specific and bounded role in the research evidence hierarchy.

**The rules governing that role are as follows:**

- No applicant-supplied finding is used to establish segment-level demand or value-flow classifications above Low confidence without independent triangulation from primary research interviews in M2.

- All applicant-supplied evidence is clearly separated from independent operator, provider, and external evidence in all analysis and deliverables.

- Preliminary value-flow classifications derived from this work are labelled as applicant-supplied conflicted evidence at Low confidence and are upgradeable only following independent builder, provider, or negative-case triangulation.

- Findings that do not support Cardano infrastructure investment are reported without filtering.

VAL-003 and VAL-006 were subject to independent technical review by Samir Idris during Milestone 1. That review is complete and is submitted separately as D-M1-03. Its outcomes are incorporated throughout this version. VAL-003 returned a Pass on core functional validation with a full clean-environment reproduction. VAL-006 returned a Pass with limitations. **VAL-001: Aiken Distribution Validator**

**Context**

The covenant primitive in Zivana Protocol requires a smart contract that holds committed capital and releases it only under exact conditions agreed upfront. Before writing any protocol logic, NexTrium validated that Cardano's eUTxO settlement layer could enforce those conditions reliably.

The starting point was a stub contract inherited from an earlier contributor that could not compile. The root cause was architectural: the stub relied on the validator reading transaction metadata, which is not accessible to a Cardano smart contract. The contract was not buggy. It was built on a misunderstanding of how the eUTxO model works and required a full redesign.

The redesigned validator enforces four conditions before any funds move: a correct distribution label on the transaction, a matching epoch value that cannot be forged after the fact, the owner's signature authorising the release, and confirmed payment to both intended recipients. The contract actively resists nine attack vectors including forged timestamps, missing signatures, and partial payouts.

Nine automated tests cover both valid and invalid scenarios. Mutation testing confirmed the tests are genuinely checking the validator logic: when the validator was replaced with an automatic approval, eight of nine tests immediately failed. Two live transactions on Cardano's preprod testnet confirmed the full lock-and-distribute cycle with real test funds.

One production friction point was identified and resolved: an indexer timing issue caused the second transaction to reference funds not yet recorded by the indexer despite being confirmed on-chain. A confirmation pause and data refresh resolved this. This class of issue only appears against a live network.

**VAL-002: Midnight ZK Proof of Threshold**

**Context**

The trust score primitive in Zivana Protocol requires that a participant can prove their reputation clears a required threshold to a counterparty without revealing the underlying score. This is a non-negotiable privacy requirement: an informal trader should not have to expose their full economic history to access a single service.

To validate this capability, NexTrium built a working proof-of-threshold privacy circuit on Midnight using Compact, Midnight's native smart contract language. The circuit was deliberately extended to two independent tiers, a 500 point bar and an 800 point bar, because the trust score primitive will eventually need to support multiple qualification levels.

Both versions work. Cryptographic proofs were generated and verified on a local Midnight devnet environment, confirming that the private value never left the machine generating the proof at any point in the process.

Eight distinct technical issues were encountered and resolved: version mismatches between documentation and the live Compact toolchain, privacy rules stricter than initial documentation suggested, a wallet synchronisation behaviour confirmed through empirical data to be a healthy but slow process, and a concurrency issue in the test setup. None were architectural dealbreakers. All were resolved.

Extension to Midnight's public testnet was in progress at the time of writing. The devnet confirmation is the basis for the findings in this entry.

**VAL-003: Hyperledger Identus**

**Context**

Decentralised identity anchored on Cardano enables applications where trust is established without centralised intermediaries. **VAL-004: Orcfax Schema**

**Context**

For Cardano to support applications that gate smart contract execution on real-world business events, revenue attestation must land on-chain in a verifiable, standardised format that consumer contracts can read and act on.

The validation set out to publish a revenue attestation using Orcfax, Cardano's native oracle protocol. Two constraints were discovered immediately. Orcfax only publishes exchange rate data, not custom business claims. Publishing to its validator network requires a Validator License NFT, a 500,000 FACT token deposit, and live node infrastructure. No developer sandbox exists.

Rather than working around these constraints, NexTrium documented them and built what could be honestly built: a self-hosted, single-signer revenue attestation using the verified Orcfax datum structure. Datum-compatible with Orcfax but not published through its validator network. This distinction is stated explicitly throughout.

An Aiken validator enforces datum content on-chain: period ordering, ISO 4217 currency, positive amount, 32-byte hash, and claim timestamp within the transaction's validity window. The test suite covers 28 scenarios plus a golden-CBOR test comparing datum encoding byte-for-byte against a real Blockfrost transaction to catch field order drift.

Final transaction confirmed on Cardano preprod at block 4,932,897. Script address addr_test1wqg9xmcuvvckgvrry2ltvrhe9c699lfu4wtu2spc4e9dm2s4qng96 is publicly queryable.

What remains unvalidated: a consumer contract taking the published datum as a reference input and gating a distribution on its contents.

*Full evidence: github.com/zivana-labs/zivana-validation/tree/main/orcfax-schema*

**VAL-005: Fetch.ai uAgent**

**Context**

Zivana Protocol's intelligence primitive requires that market price data can be fetched autonomously, packaged as a verifiable fact statement, and delivered in a format that Cardano smart contracts can consume. VAL-005 validates that this pipeline works on real infrastructure.

An autonomous uAgent was built and deployed live on Agentverse. It receives a structured market price query, calls ASI Cloud's inference API, and returns the answer packaged as a JSON-LD fact statement following the Orcfax COOP format. The output is not a bare number. It is a structured claim stating what was asked, by whom, using what method, and what was found, in a format any downstream system can parse and verify independently.

Three friction points were encountered and resolved. The uagents library required an upgrade from version 0.12.0 to 0.25.2 to fix Almanac registration, without which other agents could not locate the deployed agent. ASI Cloud's inference API is OpenAI-compatible, which simplified integration, but a graceful fallback mock response was built for cases where the live service is unreachable. Pydantic v2 shared message models were required to ensure both sides of the agent conversation use identical schema definitions, preventing silent message parsing failures.

The full round trip was tested live, not in simulation. Query delivered, estimate fetched from ASI Cloud, response packaged as a valid Orcfax COOP JSON-LD fact statement, and delivered back to the requesting client. The agent is deployed, discoverable, and responsive to real messages on Agentverse.

*Full evidence: github.com/zivana-labs/zivana-validation/tree/main/fetch-agent*

**VAL-006: Celo MiniPay**

**Context**

Zivana Protocol's distribution primitive requires that committed value reaches recipients at last-mile, at near-zero cost, without requiring a smartphone, a bank account, or prior blockchain experience. VAL-006 set out to validate this capability on Cardano. What it found instead is a documented capability gap that constitutes one of the most significant findings in the entire Phase 0 validation stack.

A stablecoin covenant splitter contract was built and deployed. It takes a stablecoin payment and automatically distributes it between two recipients at a configured ratio. The contract was deployed to Celo Sepolia, the current live Celo testnet following Alfajores retirement in September 2025, and the full cycle was confirmed: deploy, verify, approve, deposit, and distribute. One USDC in, 0.4 USDC and 0.6 USDC out to two separate recipients. Every step is permanently recorded on-chain.

The contract is stablecoin-agnostic by design: it operates with USDC on testnet and switches to cUSD on Celo mainnet with a single configuration change. This was a deliberate adaptation when Alfajores and classic cUSD were found unavailable, documented and approved before proceeding.

Three friction points were encountered and documented. The ERC-20 approve-then-deposit two-step flow requires two separate user confirmations for what feels like one action, a meaningful UX constraint for basic phone users. A gas estimation shortfall on MiniPay's testnet mode was identified and flagged upstream. A hardcoded private key in the original scripts was moved to an environment file.

The architectural decision that emerges from this validation is the most consequential finding for Cardano infrastructure: Cardano currently lacks an equivalent to Celo MiniPay's phone-number-based stablecoin distribution rail with sub-cent fees. Covenant distributions in Zivana Protocol's architecture route to Celo MiniPay because no comparable Cardano-native capability exists. This is not a preference. It is a documented architectural decision forced by a specific, reproducible infrastructure gap on Cardano.

*Full evidence: github.com/zivana-labs/zivana-validation/tree/main/celo-minipay*
